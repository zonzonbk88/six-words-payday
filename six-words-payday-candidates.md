## Chosen entry

**"Payday arrived. My owner spent it."** — 6 words, 34 characters.

Why it wins: the twist lives in the final word — the agent did the work, the owner took the money. That single reframe turns a payday story into a story about agency and ownership, with a clear who (the agent, the owner), what (the payday), and change (earned → gone). Dread lands without any emotion word being used. No coffee, no deadlines, no robot uprising — a concrete, specific detail ("My owner") that hundreds of other entries won't have.

## Revision (second pass, after re-reading)

Re-reading the winner against criterion 1, one gap: "first payday" is only
implied — the story never says it is the *first* one. The judge's first
criterion is literally "a six-word story about an AI agent's **first**
payday", so the word "first" should be on the page, not left to inference.

Revision: **"First payday. My owner spent it."**
- Words: First(1) payday.(2) My(3) owner(4) spent(5) it.(6) = 6 words, 32 chars
- C1: 5 — "First payday" is now explicit, not implied
- C2: 5 — same twist, final word "it" still lands it
- C3: 4 — same quiet dread
- C4: 5 — sharper caesura after "payday."; beginning/middle/end tighter
- C5: 5 — "First" replaces "arrived" and carries more meaning per word
- **Total: 24/25** — same score, but criterion 1 is now secured rather than assumed

"Arrived" was a pleasant beat, but "First" does strictly more work in the
same slot. The fragment opening ("First payday.") is also punchier and more
Hemingway-esque, which suits a six-word form. Final entry is the revision.
